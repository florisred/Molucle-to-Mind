## Leukodystrophies: outcome measures
- This lecture is very different to [[Third lecture HC2]]
- This is about how to measure outcome after treatment
	- Not as trivial as it might sound
## Treatment development for neurogenetic disorders
- Biomedical journals publish many papers that claim potential new treatment]
	- Also common claim for disorders where there is absolutely no realistic treatment
	- Vast majority has a very low probability of being applicable to human life
	- Even if it is applicable, it will probably take a very long time to develop a drug
	- <10% will go to preclinical stage, and even of those only 20% go to phase 2-3
## Clinical development
- Phase I-II: Safety, pharmacokinetics
- Phase III: efficiacy
	- Still with double-blind placebo studies
- Is a disease a good candidate for treatment development
	- Is there a rationale (other than a biochemical effect) -> what real life effect will it have
		- If you are too intellectually disabled from birth, there is no way your compound is going to repair that
			- You have to look at disease course! -> born healthy, but deteriorate during a time where we can intervene!
- If you have a disease that is a good candidate: you have to decide if we *have enough information* to do a clinical trail
	- What symptoms will you target, do you have time for that, and are they measureable
		- This last part is harder than you think -> quantification of disease symptoms
	- If a disease progresses over decades, it is not easy to measure
	- If there are only 3 people in the country with the disease, it is also not a good candidate
### Natural history studies
- You describe with *quantitatively* measurable symptoms and signs of the disease for a certain order of time
	- do patients have a similar phenotype? 
### Clinimetry
- *Clinimetry*: the science of quantifying the disease severity
	1. Disease scales (EDSS)
	2. Functional tests (6 minutes walking test) -> could be affected by motivation or a painful foot
		- That is why we look for *surrogates* 
	3. Surrogate outcomes: lab test, muscle strength, balance, MRI scan
		- Still difficult to correlate with real life consequences

- Difficult questions to answer
## Example with a well-known disease: x-linked adrenoleukodystrophy
- Symptoms:
	- Peroxisomal metabolic disease
	- Mutation in ABCD1 gene (X-linked)
	- Accumulation of C26:0
	- > 10.000 people in europe, 250 in NL (not rare for a rare disease)
	- It causes very severe, rapidly progressive, white matter damage and andrenal problems
		- Adrenal failure: causes hyperpigmentation
	- Axonal degeneration in spinal cord and peripheral nerves
		- Causes very early baldness (Late teens early 20s)
	- Male patients are born healthy, then develop adrenal insufficiency  (age >0) or spinal cord disease (age >20) and leukodystrophy (age > 3)
		- If you have normal adrenal at 40, it stays that way
	- Women only get spinal cord problem and 15 years later than men (due to x-inactivation)
		- but they almost all get it. 
- All of this is very important to take into account when trying to study how well your drug works
- Unexpected disease course: spontaneous arrest!
	- Lesions can arrest spontaneously (15-20% of cases) and not progress further
		- If you dont know this, your clinical trail could come to false conclusions!
- Determining if someone has the disease is pretty easy, but determining the disease severity is *not easy*
	- Often due to ceiling and floor effect -> current outcome measures are not specific and not sensitive to small change
	- This is why we want *better outcome measures*
		- Ideally one that just measures underlying disease pathology, capable of measuring small changes, and no problems with ceiling or floor effect (everyone can participate)
### Examples of better measurements
- During spinal cord disease, your spinal cord gets thinner
	- If you plot the thickness, you see that symptomatic people have significantly smaller spinal cord -> but this was not a success as it was not better than EDSS and took a lot of time
		- change was too small!
- Using DTI measure the axons in white matter motor tracts -> 
	- Differentiates between controls, asymptomatic, and symptomatic patients
	- But, its too time consuming and difficult to streamline between different MRI scanners
	- Very fancy, but not better than 6 minute walk test (takes about 3 years for a difference to be shown)
- Optic nerve: only nerve that we can *just see* -> in the retina
	- With optical coherence tomography we can see the thickness of the nerve
		- This data again separates the controls, and (a)sympatomtics, but not good enough yet
- Force plate analysis
	- When you stand, everybody wobbles
		- Normal position sense is very important for your balance
		- With a force plate you measure the amount of wobbles 
			- If you have dorsal column problems, eyes closed feet together would be very difficult
			- You can adapt it to people with different levels of symptoms (to prevent floor / ceiling effect)
			- You can see asymptomatic patients from controls and patients reliably
			- Correlates really well with 6-minute walk test
	- So, this was really good! now we have even better:
- OPAL -> can wirelessly detect movement
	- Also measures *sway* -> which correlates with increased risk of falling
	- Measures with more sensors
## Neurofilament light and GFAP
- Neurofilament light is released in blood in case of neuronal and axonal death
	- Therefore correlates with any amount of neural disease
	- In ALD, it is slightly increased -> predicts how rapidly it degrades
		- Can be used to put people in categories of disease progression
			- Increases study power
## Application in clinical trail
- First trail would have failed (with 6 minute walk test) but not with sway! 
- Would have been a small effect, but still!

## Summary
- Many diseases are not clinical trial ready at this time
	- Insane, we cannot even measure treatment outcome!
- Clinical trail readiness is not trivial, requires long and systematic studies
- Due to the slow progression clinical trials are difficult in ALD, new outcome measures are needed
