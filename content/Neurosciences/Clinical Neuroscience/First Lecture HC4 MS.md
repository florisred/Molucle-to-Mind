## Symptoms of MS
- Visual problems
	- Optic neuritis
	- Pain behind the eye
	- Decreased colour vision
	- Decreased visual acuity, especially central visual fields
	- Diplopia -> double vision
- Pyramidal symptoms
	- Spastic gait -> a leg is a bit stiff, not fluid, more difficult
		- Due to laesions in spinal cord or internal capsule
- Sensory disturbances
	- TIngling / painful sensations 
	- Numbness
	- Symptom of Lhermitte -> electric shock when bending
		- Due to small demyelinating laesion in the spinal cord
- In advanced stages:
	- Bladder problems -> urine retention --> cannot pee but really really need to
	- Bowel problems
	- Sexual problems
- Ataxia -> we've all experienced this when we were drunk af
	- Looks more like a balance problem
- Cognitive disturbances
	- Memory (not often, only hippocampal demyelination)
	- Other problems, (attention, concentration) due to processing speed
- Fatigue
	- High prevalence
	- No good treatment
	- Unknown cause
	- Less visible
- Depresson -> 50%
- Also big impact on relation ships
## Different MS subtypes
- Old way of thinking:
	- Clinically isolated syndrome
		- Relapsing-remitting syndrome (RRMS) -> 85%
			- Short periods when it gets worse, then no changes
			- often progresses into SPMS
		- Secondary progressive -> SPMS -> 80%
			- First RRMS, then becomes worse and worse gradually
		- Primary progressive (PPMS) -> 10-15%
			- Constant decline from the beginning
- Radiologically isolated syndrome
- RAW: relapse associated worsening, like in RRMS
- PIRA -> progression independent of relapse activity (even between relapses, you can also have slow neurological decline)
	- Patients with RRMS can also have PIRA

### How do you diagnose it?
- Think of 4 things
	1. Are the clinical features typical of demyelination
	2. Is there objective evidence of demyelination -> imaging 
		- YOU HAVE TO USE MRI OTHERWISE YOU DIE (or just have a chance of a false positive)
	3. Is there a high risk of a subsequent demyelinating event? -> dissemination in time
		- This is called a relapse (clinical attack, focal inflammation)
	4. Is there no other, better, explanation for the clinical presentation
- DiT -> Dissemination in Time
- DiS -> Dissemination in Space
	- Where are the laesions that lead to MS diagnosis?
	- Optic nerve, (juxta)cortical, periventrical, brainstem, spinal cord
- Clinical DiS and DiT
	- In three different areas, a patient may expericence three different dissemination -> DiT
#### Imaging in MS - MRI
- **DiT**
	- Disease activity
		- T2 lesions (white problems)
	- Irreversible damage
		- T1 lesions -> black holes (bad)
	- Active lesions
		- T1 + contrast -> enhancing lesions
			- Lesions that are currently inflamed, others that are not
			- This is proof of DiT -> Right now there is one actively inflamed, but others are not
	- This means that you need either a contrast scan with active and inactive lesions, or a new T2 scan with more lesions after a few months
- **DiS**
	-  Like 2-5 different places
- **What do lesions look like in MS?**
	- Number, size, and distribution vary widely
	- Typically ovoid shaped, perivascular orientation
	- often looks like a white dot, but not every white spot is a lesion -> prone to false positives
	- Localisation
		- Periventricular  -> easier, kind of possible with computers
		- Juxtacorticla / cortical -> harder to see, not possible with computers
		- Infratentorial
		- Spinal cord
- **Other uses MRI**
	- Ruling out other diseases -> differential diagnosis
		- In older patients, vascular disease *could* look similar
			- But not with straight angle to ventricles
		- Sarcoidosis -> in spinal cord, but lesion looks way longer (3-4 vertebrae)
	- Monitoring disease activity
