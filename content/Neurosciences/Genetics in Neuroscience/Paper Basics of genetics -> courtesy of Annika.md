### **_Basic genetics_**

**Mutation =** alteration in the DNA coding sequence.

- Deletion
	o   Lack of expression
	o   Leads to a frameshift -> early stop codon
	o   Truncated peptide is inactive and degraded in the cell -> no protein expression
- Insertion
	o   If 3 amino acids are inserted -> insertion of additional amino acids
	- Insertion of nonintegral multiple of 3 -> frameshift
	o   Two kinds of insertions:
		§  Inversions
		§  Translocation
- Rearrangement
- Single base changes
	o   Silent mutation: no effect because it expresses the same amino acid
	o   Conservative mutation: new amino acid has similar chemical properties to the original amino acid
	o   Missense mutation: gain of function or loss of function
	o   Stop mutation: premature termination of translation  lack of expression
	o   Frameshift insertion

**X-linked trait**
·      Transmitted from females to half their offspring
·      Male transmits to all of his daughters but none of his sons
·      **X-linked recessive:**
	o   Tends to be expressed in mails (only one X chromosome)
	o   Possible for females to express, but rare
·      **X-linked dominant:**
	o   Affect males and females
	o   Some are lethal in males and therefore only seen in females
**Single gene transmission (rare):**
·      Recessive mutations are most often loss of function changes. common for enzyme deficiencies: because of the catalytic nature of enzymes, loss of 50% of the enzyme is tolerated, but complete loss of function leads to product deficiency.
·      Dominant mutations are more diverse: some cause loss of function, some cause gain of function.
·      Individuals with a mutant genotype do not always express the phenotype = **nonpenetrance.** If the disorder only appears with the passage of time, the penetrance is said to be age dependent. Nonpenetrance can happen when a dominant disorder s kips a generation (child has affected grandparent, but no affected parent).
·      Genetic traits can display variable expressivity (differences in the degree or quality of expression of a phenotype). **Anticipation** happens if the trait has the tendency to become more severe with each generation. Happens in neurological disorders caused by triplet repeat expansions (Huntington).
·      **Imprinting =** the differential expression of maternal and paternal alleles for a particular gene. Whether this mutation causes a change in phenotype depends on whether the mutation occurs on the allele of the parent that is expressed in the offspring.
·      **Mitochondrial inheritance =** mutations present as failure in energy metabolism. These mutations are maternally-inherited. If there is a mixture of normal and mutant DNAs in the same cell, **heteroplasmy** happens: mitochondria segregate to the daughter cells, which can lead to multiple cell populations with various proportions of mutant and wildtype mitochondrial DNA  differences in expression of the trait in different tissues or individual members of the same family

**Multifactorial inheritance:**
·      Multiple genes interacting with one another and with environmental factors are required to cause the phenotype.
·      Can be studied by clustering families (not in accordance with Mendelian genetics), and twin studies.

**Polymorphic locus =** a locus with multiple alleles in which the frequency of the least common allele is 1% or greater. Types of polymorphisms:
·      Variations in individual DNA bases: **single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)**
·      Variations in the number of repeated sequences

Balance between mutation and selection: under certain circumstances the deleterious allele might confer an advantage (might have been in a different era and under different environmental conditions than exist today). For recessive traits, this selective advantage might apply to heterozygotes, with the homozygotes being at a relative disadvantage.

**Founder effect =** when some genetic variants occur more commonly in specific population either due to local environmental conditions that favor particular alleles or a mutant has been introduces into a small population in which breeding occurs only among members of that population for many generations.

Relating genes to effects on health and disease can be done through **genetic linkage studies:** based on the fact genes are arrayed in a linear order on the chromosomes. A specific pare of alleles in two closely linked genes tends to segregate together from generation to generation unless a **crossover** occurs at meiosis between the loci. The frequency of such crossover events is a function of the distance between the loci, so measuring _recombination rates_ between adjacent loci provides a genetic map.

The genetic linkage approach works well if a single gene contributes to a disorder in a major way, but is less suited to discovery of genes that make more modest contributions to multifactorial disorders  
	-> needs **genetic association analysis**.
·      Testing  ‘candidate’ polymorphisms
·      Case-control study in which the frequency of a specific allele is compared between populations of affected and nonaffected individuals.
·      Linkage disequilibrium = the allele itself contributes to the cause of the disease or it is located nearby another allele that is the causative factor.

### **_Neurogenetics_**
Most neuropsychiatric disorders have not yielded predominant biochemical abnormalities (such as plaques in AD) and don’t have Mendelian forms of the disease. Nonetheless, these neuropsychiatric disorders run in families, and twin and adoption studies show them to be highly influenced by genes.

In older genetic studies, the hypothesis was often that each psychiatric disorder represented a natural, independent category. However, some psychiatric disorders co-occur in the same families. That is because these disorders share a high percentage of risk-associated genes with each other.

The DSM (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders) defines disorders in terms of fairly arbitrary constellations of symptoms, because they often lack objective biomarkers.

Genetics might contribute to psychiatric diagnoses, but the pleiotropy of many risk-associated genes suggests that genetics alone will not prove sufficient to make diagnoses for common psychiatric disorders.

Linkage analysis failed for psychiatric disorders when it was successful for many diseases with simple mendelian patterns of inheritance because the genetic architecture of psychiatric disorders may be more complex.

Furthermore, individuals affected by psychiatric disorders may carry many alleles that influence their risk. These risk variants may aggregate in individuals and families by chance = polygenic trait  violates assumptions underlying linkage analysis.

Candidate gene studies ask whether a particular allele of a nominated gene is statistically  associated with a disease by comparing genotypes of affected subjects to those of healthy control subjects. The underlying assumption was that the molecular biology of mental disorders was well enough understood to make it possible to nominate candidate genes. However, modern genome-wide studies have not found evidence for association for most of these candidate loci.

**Newer techniques:**
·      Completion of the Human Genome Project: found a remarkable degree of variation among individuals.
·      Technological innovation: can more easily find SNPs.-> makes it cheaper to sequence
·      A scientific understanding of the _scale_ required to be successful:
	o   The heritability of psychiatric disorders appears to be distributed across many hundreds of loci, with each individual locus explaining only a small fraction of the overall heritability of the phenotype.
	o   GWAS comes with the burden of multiple hypothesis testing (threshold of 5x10-8)

**Why is it challenging to come up with a biological interpretation for the results from CVAS?**
·      Modest effects of the haplotypes.
·      Different combinations in different genomes
·      Functional alleles not yet known
·      Noncoding variants and regulatory DNA
**Rare-variant association study (RVAS):**
·      Challenge: the human genome contains thousands of variants that alter or terminate protein sequences, complicating the assignment of disease causality to any particular variant.
·      Challenge in psychiatric disorders: only partial penetrance for adult-onset psychiatric phenotypes.
·      Studies based on sequencing are expanded to more patients and controls, so a growing set of genes is likely to be implicated by allelic series of rare variants -> presents an opportunity for biological study by permitting measurement of functional effects across these alleles.

Challenge of polygenicity for neurobiology: current approaches must be complemented by new approaches that permit far higher throughput, that are more inductive in nature, and that permit analysis of biological systems beyond a single hypothesis about a putatively causal allele.

### **_Genome-wide association studies (GWAS)_**
Aim to identify associations of genotypes with phenotypes by testing for differences in the allele frequency of genetic variants between individuals who are ancestrally similar but differ phenotypically.

GWAS typically reports blocks of correlated SNPs that all show a statistically significant association with the trait of interest (genomic risk loci).

Uses for GWAS results:

·      Find control variables in epidemiology studies to account for confounding genetic group differences.

·      Predict an individual’s risk for physical and mental disease based on their genetic profile  polygenic risk scores (PRSs)

Challenge in GWAS: interpreting these associations in a biological and genomic context. Most traits are influenced by thousands of causal variants that individually confer very little risk, are often associated with many other traits, and are correlated with causal and non-causal variants that are physically close as a result of **linkage disequilibrium**, making direct biological, causal inferences complicated. Furthermore, genetic associations may differ across ancestries, complicating direct comparisons between groups of individuals.

Steps of conducting GWAS:

1.        Selecting study populations

2.        Genotyping

3.        Data processing  account for ancestry and relatedness to avoid false positives or negatives and biased test statistics owing to population stratification

4.        Testing for associations  often with linear or logistic regression models which include covariates such as age, sex, and ancestry to avoid confounding effects from demographic factors. Furthermore, you should consider that genotypes of genetic variants that are physically close together are not independent as they tend to be in linkage disequilibrium.

5.        Accounting for false discovery  results in a Bonferroni testing threshold of P < 5 x 10-8

6.        Meta-analysis  increases sample size by analyzing data from multiple cohorts together.

Populations used in GWAS:

·      **Population-based GWAS:** individuals are assumed to be a random draw from the population. Common design is a case-control study. If cases and controls are not genotypes together on the same chip, extra effort must be made during quality control and subsequent analyses to minimize artefacts.

·      **Family-based GWAS:** use first-degree relatives. Require larger sample sizes than GWAS of unrelated individuals to achieve the same statistical power, but avoid issues with population stratification. Typically use variations on the transmission disequilibrium test to examine the segregation of an allele within a family. This method can substantially boost power for some traits, especially when studying late-onset diseases for which the collection of large data sets is challenging. However, it may rely on self-reported family history, which may not always be accurate.

·      **Isolated populations:** key advantage is that otherwise rare functional variants may be present in higher frequencies (leads to increased power for such variants). Tend to have high genetic homogeneity owing to the extinction of alleles through genetic bottlenecks. However, discoveries in these populations might be hard to replicate in other populations.

·      **Biobanks:** contain data from thousands of genotyped individuals who have been deeply phenotyped through questionnaires, laboratory measurements
