## Why are we all different? - the central question of genetics
- Basic question
 - Genes shape who you are, your behavior and characteristics
 - Another question: *What makes some people more alike than others*
	 - Central question here: *Nature vs. Nurture*
		 - *Nature*: its all genetics
		 - *Nurture*: same way of raising, same culture, same etc.
		 - Are families more alike due to same environment or due to genetics
		 - No longer is either or, but used to be that way
### Twin studies
- Why is it so difficult whether its nature or nurture?
	- Because they influence each other
	- It's hard to separate people who are genetically similar to each other
		- Offspring are raised by their parents -> both nature and nurture
- We can separate this a little though! -> two different types of twins
	- *Monozygotic twins* -> genetically identical + same environment
	- *Fraternal twins* -> different genetics (50%) + same environment
- You can also split the environment (different experiences, same experience + different reaction)
- If we compare the trait correlation (divided by two) then we can get an estimate of heritability
	- G = 2(r<sub>MZ</sub>-r<sub>DZ</sub>)
	- E = 1 - r<sub>MZ</sub> -> (unique environment)
	- C = 1 - G - E (common environment)
- Does *not* rely on a perfect understanding of the environment
	- We use it as a throwaway term (everything that is different, including measurement error)
	- Nice thing about twins is that age differences do not factor in -> they are the same age
## Heritability
- **Heritability** is the proportion of *trait variance* between individuals that is attributable to *genetic variance* between individuals
- Causes genetically related individuals to correlate on a trait 
- If we measure two people at random on heritable traits, one would not be a predictor for another
- This is not the case in heritable traits with related people! 
- *Heritability* is what causes genetically related people to be correlated on that trait
- Average heritability is about 50%
	- Both nature and nurture were right!
### Is heritability destiny?
- Are there 0% heritable traits? How about 50% and 100%?
- There was quite some discussion on this in class, showing the complexity of the concept heritability
	- Sometimes if you have an allele that makes you destined, but you could die before you get it or there could be a cure
	- Down syndrome would be 100%? But we are not talking about a single person, but about an entire population
	- Heritability is *context dependent* and *population dependent*
- *Phenotypic heterogeneity* can also complicate this!!! -> we will talk more about this later
- **pleiotropy**
- Language would be non-heritable
### 50 Years of twin studies
- Main conclusion:
	- All traits are heritable ot some extent
	- Influence of C<sup>2</sup> is relatively small -> *family / common environment*
	- majority of traits are consistent with a model where all genetic variance is additive
- Suggests that variations in genes underlie trait differences between individuals
	- Logical next step: *which genes??*
## How similar are organisms and humans in DNA?
- bananas: 50%
- Mice: 87,5%
- Chimps: 99%
- Two different humans: 99.9%
	- This 0.1% explains *all difference* that we know between humans
- Genetic variations can occur
	- in coding (gene) -> 1.5% of DNA
	- Exons (non coding) -> 99% of DNA
- Genetic variations cvan be:
	- Harmless, Harmful, Helpful, Latent (dependent on other gene or environment), and silent
## Monogenic & polygenic disorders
- *Monogenic* -> a single change in base pair that has a big effect
	- Highly *penetrant*, most genetic causes already know
	- Also called *Mendelian* disease
- *Polygenic disorders* -> many small effects that add up to a disease
	- Behavior is very complex, that involves *higher-order* functions
		- Many things working together
- Evolution shapes this! -> redundancy is good.

## Law of independent assortment
- For the 7 studied traits in peas, they were *independent of each other*
	- Two different phenotypes have no influence on each other
- This is not very true always, causal alleles can be very close to each other on the chromosomes
	- Due to *Linkage Disequilibrium* -> instead of inherited separately, closely linked together
	- Less likely to be affected by DNA *recombination*
## Discovering genes for complex traits
- The above was where we were *before we had the human genome*
- We started with *candidate gene studies*
	- Choose a variant *a priori* due to what we thought we knew already
	- Compare allele frequencies between cases and controls
	- But, most of these were probably false positives -> small sample sizes, low power
## Linkage analysis
- Somewhat genome wide, looking at quite a few polymorphisms
	- See whether they segregated within a disease within families
- Little better approach, linkage was useful in alcohol dependency
- Only limited, since you have to have families with similar diseases
## GWAS
- Possible due to a lot of tech advances
	- If you have little fragments of DNA, you can take their DNA that have a lot of probe sides
	- called micoarrays
- You can see which alleles contribute slightly to higher disease chances
- Hypothesis free whole genome screen
	- Benefits: No p-hacking (many tests, only report the statistically significant ones)
	- Drawbacks:  need a large sample size, lots of tests, no hypothesis, hard to interpret
- Around 2005 they started, now there are manyyyy many
	- Turns out that a lot of the genome is important for health and behavior
- Lead to a revolution in genetics
	- There is no single gene for depression!
	- To be able to detect the small contributions single genes have on genes many sources are pooling their data -> more than a million is pretty common
### Common disease common variant hypothesis
- There is a general pattern for how heritability works
	- Higher penetrance -> higher likelihood you have the disease if you have that allele
	- Most of the genes we see fall in a certain band
		- Mendelian disease -> very rare allele, but high penetrance
		- Low frequency variants with intermediate penetrance
		- Most varients discovered by GWAS -> very common, but low penentrance
		- If its really common, and highly penetrant, many people will get sick early before getting children -> low prevalence
			- Except for late-onset diseases
- Most of the variance we see in GWAS are not functional
	- They dont have a really clear function, most of the time they are in non-coding genes
- Most of the variances have very small effects -> not good for experimental manipulation
- All of these challenges we will talk about in the coming weeks
## Designing functional follow-up experiments
- knock-out, over/under expression, mRNA expression
- Two functional follow-up strategies
	- use functional information from GWAS  to gain insight on higher-level biological pathways / mechanisms
	- Use techniques that allow targeting multiple variants / genes simultaneously
