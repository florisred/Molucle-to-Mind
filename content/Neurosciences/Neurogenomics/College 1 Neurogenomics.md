## Why do neurogenomics?
Well, on every level of neuroscience, it is important to at least have a basic understanding of proteins and the genome. Also, Most therapies apply to the level of proteins. 
Genes are an important starting point, as we have tried to get to understand the common disorders in other methods (for the last 100+ years) hasn't resulted in much. With genes you can learn a lot more. For example, the estimated heritability of schizophrenia is pretty high. We are going to try to use that information (which genes are important) to understanding and hopefully modifying the disease. 
There are over 200 loci that contribute to schizophrenia. So at least 200 genes that contribute to the disease. *my take: maybe it is less useful to look at the specific genes, as they do not have to overlap. We could also look at the system, as many different causes result in similar disease. Maybe the disease vulnerability might be more in the system itself than the genes.*

## How do you go to systems biology?
You go from less to more complex levels. You start with the molecules, then go onto organelle. We know quite a bit about organelles, because they are generally pretty (relatively) simple. You can then try to see if we can predict the behavior of the synapse, neurons, or even higher levels. 
You start with mutations, and then slowly try to see what effects that has on proteins, then cells, then circuits, then systems, and then finding out how that relates to symptoms. This takes years, maybe decades, but is worthwhile. 
If you can create computer simulations of signal transduction, you can quantitatively predict how variations we all have could contribute to different outcomes. 
Important here is to consider the tri-partite of the brain. Next to the synapse, glia are also very important!
