## Disease modeling
Auguste Deter (1901) was delusional and could not remember anything. She died on 1906, and was actually the first patient ever diagnosed with alzheimer's. After more than 100 years, we still have no serious disease modifying treatments. 
Alzheimer's is characterized by amyloid plaques and tau tangles. Tau is a microtubule binding protein, to make it more stable. In AD, the Tau becomes hyperphosphorylated, which turns it aggregation-prone. 
We actually think that AD is caused by the amyloid cascade hypothesis.  This is the idea that you have a mutation that leads to more amyloid beta production, which then leads to tau pathology, then leading to neurodegeneration. However, only 1% of the people have familial AD (with very high penetrance), so not everyone has that basic mutation. Sporadic AD probably is caused by less severe, more dispersed mutation. 
Healthy subjects can actually also have a high amyloid load. Furthermore, tangles actually correlate way better with the amount of neurodegeneration and the onset of dementia than the amyloid plaques do. This is why some people argue that amyloid is the trigger, and tau is the bullet. 
Interestingly, tau starts in the hippocampus (memory), and amyloid really in some cortical structures. That is interesting. We have developed drugs that stopped amyloid production, which did not do much, and removed amyloid (very good at that), which also did not do anything. There is no improvement of cognition or slowing of cognition decline. 
Some people think this is because treatment is started too late, but yeah, its a whole controversy. 

## Dual cascade hypothesis
So if removing amyloid doesnt do much, it is likely that the amyloid cascade hypothesis is at least flawed. Therefore, I present to you, dear audience, the dual-cascade hypothesis!
Firstly, why is it so hard to do alzheimer research? Well, we cannot look at the brains of people who would develop alzheimers. If a person is pre-symptomatic, we could never find out if they were going to develop AD. Brain is also too complex to do a simple biopsy or something. We tried to study with a lot of mice, but it turns out they might not be the best model. Mice normally do not get AD, so we have to induce very specific genes to show tau- and amyloid pathology. 
Now, we can do patient-derived IPSC neurons. They are an unlimited resource, so we can do all we want with them. They have endogenous expression of the AD-causing genes.

## Induced pluripotent stem cells
You take a skin cell and reintroduce 'yamanaka' or reprogramming factors. You take them from embryonic stem cells. Giving them to skin cells reprogram (or rejuvenate) the cells into pluripotency. They cannot make every cell (for example eggs), but many others are possible. 
So, the next logical question is whether or not neurons replicate AD disease in a dish. If so, you can study it like crazily well! It turns out that there is more Abeta production. What about Tau?
Well, there is also more tau. This is a good case of correlation, not causation. They found that APP precursor (causing lower Ab) also results in lower tau. This is a point for the Amyloid cascade hypothesis. However, maybe the amyloid beta isn't disease causing, but the C99 fragment causes tau. Both are byproducts of too much Ab production, but only C99 is probably toxic. This also explains why we didn't find any DMTs when reducing Ab in tested drugs!

## Mechanisms of statins in AD
Statins normally reduce cholesterol. It was found that cholesterol causes cholesteryl esters to appear, which in turn cause pTau to exist. It was then also found that (logically) more statins reduce tau a lot! The problem with statins is that they do not pass the BBB, but Efaviernz turns out to do that. Now there are trails with this!
There are two different hypothesis: Amyloid cascade hypothesis  states that the cholesterol causes Ab which then causes Tau, and dual upstream regulator hypothesis says that cholesterol has a direct effect on both! 

## Limitations of IPSC models
There are many more cells than just neurons in the brain, while we are only able to make immature gliacells and neurons. They remain very similar to the fetal stage, and there is a lot of differentiation between patients.
