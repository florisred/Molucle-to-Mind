## Local translation
In [[Neurons]], dendrites and axons can be very far away from the nucleus and the soma and can still have thousands of synapses. If all proteins would be made in in the soma, and they had to be transported to the outer ends, it could take up to 10 days to accomplish that. This is because certain axons can be up to 1 meter long! Therefore, a lot of proteins are translated locally at the synapse. 
This does not only happen in neurons, but in all cells. It is also a highly conserved mechanism. It also helps with having spatiotemporal control over proteins in subcellular compartments. This means that in cells, RNA must be present outside of the soma. 70% of mRNAs localize to specific subcellular compartments. 
We have actually known this for quite a while. In 1988 it was found that the mRNA for cytoskeletons was present in dendrites. So this is not news. 

## The axon is an autonomous structure
In an experiment, the axons was severed from its soma. It could still find its target, even without proteins from there. It will eventually die without its body, but it can do things autonomously.
This works because of local synthesis. It works with contact-mediated repulsion and attraction, and chemo-attraction and chemo-repulsion. This causes changes in protein synthesis, guiding the axon. The first clue that it depended on local protein synthesis was due to adding a drug that stops protein synthesis. This does not happen anymore when you block protein synthesis. 
After stimulation, mRNA appears on the side of the stimulus after a few minutes. This means that the mRNA moved locally. Stimulation also stimulates beta-actin translation on the side of the stimulation. We know that actin is responsible for the cytoskeleton, so this makes a lot of sense. The axon 'moves' due to changes in its cytoskeleton, which are regulated by which side the stimulus/guidance signal comes from! easy!

## RNA transport to neurons
If you have a naked and exposed mRNA in the cytoplasm, it will rapidly be degraded. So you have to have something on it to stop it from slowly dying a horrible death.  So how are they transported? Using RNA-binding-Protein (RBP)-granules. They can separate from the rest of the cell environment (like a vesicle or organelle). They regulate many functions relating to RNA in the nucleus and cytoplasm, and do not have a membrane. They are more like protein complexes. We could call them membraneless organelles. 
So how are the mRNA Granules transported? They could bind directly to the motor protein on the cytoskeleton. Otherwise, they use a more energy-efficient method, called hitchhiking. They bind onto the late [[Endocytic pathways|endosome]] or lysosome, which in turn is connected to kinesin or dynin (Cioni et al, 2019). 

## Which mRNAs are localized and translated in axons and dendrites?
In most cases, you isolate the dendrite (or axon) and then just do RNA sequencing. This way you find out exactly which mRNAs are present there. It turned out that there are very many. We do not know if they are all actually expressed, but you would assume that they are. Otherwise it would be strange to find them there, lol. 
The main categories of mRNA proteins found in axons are numerous, including transcription factors. This is done to signal things back to the nucleus. We are mostly going to focus on ribosomal proteins though. This is one of the most common found mRNAs in axons, in all stages of development. This is kind of strange, because ribosomes are only made in the nucleus. 
Briefly: Done with pulsed SILAC which labels proteins that have recently been made. Then, ribosomes were purified. And then they tried to see if amino-acids are in the ribosomes, which is what they found. Only the ribosomal proteins on the outside of the complex showed this, probably to repair damaged ribosomes. You need to synthesize ribosomal proteins to keep the ribosome healthy to sustain local protein synthesis. If you inhibit local ribosome translation, the protein translation is inhibited a lot.
